# EDA v1

**Charts** (in `figures/`, each with a headline title and a caption). 01_u5mr_trends.png: "Under-5 mortality fell in all five countries, but Pakistan stays highest" (modelled UN IGME estimates, 2000-2023). 02_missingness_map.png: "Education and birth-care data have large gaps, worst for Sri Lanka's enrolment" (dark means a value is present). 03_enrolment_vs_u5mr.png: "Higher female enrolment goes with lower child mortality, pooled and within countries" (pooled line on the left, one line per country on the right). 04_log_transform.png: "Logging GDP per capita pulls in the long right tail". 05_prediction_time_split.png: "Models predict 2016-23 mortality well when trained on earlier years"

**Transformation.** GDP per capita is right-skewed (skew 1.45); after taking the natural log the skew is 0.58 (chart 04). The reason is that mortality falls roughly with the proportional, not absolute, rise in income, and the log stops Sri Lanka and the highest years dominating the fit

**Five properties.** Structure: the raw sheet is a wide country-indicator by year table, which we reshaped into a 120-row country-year panel with 14 indicators. Granularity: each value is a national average for one year, so provinces, rural-urban gaps and the sex of the child are invisible. Scope: five South Asian countries over 2000-2023, with Sri Lanka far below the others, so results do not generalise to other regions. Temporality: annual and heavily trending, and some series (fertility, mortality) are smoothed or interpolated, which means neighbouring years are not independent. Faithfulness: mortality is a modelled estimate, enrolment is a gross ratio that can overstate participation, and unemployment is modelled with an apparent break in Pakistan

**Confounder check (female secondary enrolment vs mortality).** The pooled correlation is -0.93 on 98 rows, and within each country it is -0.83 (Bangladesh), -0.99 (India), -0.97 (Nepal), -0.97 (Pakistan) and +0.41 for Sri Lanka, whose 12 years sit in a narrow 90-99% range. Regressing mortality on enrolment gives -1.02 deaths per 1,000 for each extra percentage point; adding country fixed effects leaves it at -1.05, so differences between countries are not what drives it. Adding log GDP shrinks it to -0.28, and adding a year trend as well brings it to -0.14 (-0.19 with country effects and year only; -0.23 when Sri Lanka is dropped). So the story changes: most of the raw association disappears once development and time are held fixed, though the sign stays negative. This is association from shared trends, not evidence that education causes lower mortality.

**Prediction baseline.** Using ten development indicators (all 120 rows), a model trained on 2000-2015 and tested on 2016-2023 predicts mortality with a mean absolute error of 3.8 deaths per 1,000 (ridge, R2 0.94) or 3.4 (random forest, R2 0.94), against 26.2 for guessing the training mean. When a whole country is held out, the errors are much larger (ridge 15.5, random forest 11.8; R2 0.55 and 0.69), and the ridge error is 30.3 when Nepal is held out. Adding female enrolment on the 98 complete rows does not improve either test (time split: ridge MAE 3.9 to 4.4, forest 2.9 to 2.8; held-out country: ridge 15.4 to 15.6, forest 8.3 to 8.8).

**Three findings in plain words.** First, child mortality fell sharply in all five countries between 2000 and 2023, from 107.9 to 57.8 deaths per 1,000 in Pakistan (down 46%) and from 91.8 to 28.0 in India (down 69%). Second, countries and years with more girls in secondary school have lower child mortality, but most of that link fades once we account for countries getting richer over time, so we cannot say education itself is responsible. Third, the models predict the future of these five countries well but are much less reliable for a country they have not seen.
